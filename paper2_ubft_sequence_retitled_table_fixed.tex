\begin{abstract}
This paper studies monetary-policy intervention as a boundary condition in a term-structure model of instantaneous rolling forward rates. The central observation is that policy acts most directly at the short end of the curve, while longer maturities remain market-determined. This asymmetry is represented by imposing boundary conditions at zero remaining maturity and deriving the resulting admissible maturity profiles of drift, stochastic loading, and variance.

The analysis compares three boundary regimes: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item a Neumann condition represents a free short end, 
    \item a Dirichlet condition represents the limiting case of a pinned policy boundary and, 
    \item a Robin condition represents partial policy anchoring, in which the short-end slope responds to deviations from a policy reference rate. 
\end{itemize}
In the repeated-root branch $r=1$, the boundary-value problem generates the linear-exponential maturity mode
\[
\tau e^{-a^2\tau},
\]
which provides a structural derivation of humped volatility rather than assuming such a shape exogenously.

The paper shows that policy boundaries do not merely affect local short-rate behaviour, they determine the admissible propagation of stochastic exposure across the maturity curve. The Robin case gives the economically relevant intermediate regime: monetary policy moderates short-rate movements without fully eliminating short-end randomness. The resulting maturity loadings provide a boundary-condition interpretation of humped volatility specifications used in the term-structure literature. In the UBFT sequence, this paper supplies the short-boundary classification used by the completed two-boundary geometry developed in the sequel.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Monetary policy is implemented at the short end of the interest-rate curve often through central-bank instruments that set or steers overnight rates, reserve remuneration, and operating targets, but they does not mechanically set the entire forward curve. Longer maturities remain market objects, reflecting expectations, credibility, term premia, and uncertainty about future policy. This paper studies the mathematical consequence of that asymmetry.

This is the second paper in the Unified Boundary Field Theory (UBFT) sequence for instantaneous rolling forward rates (IRFRs). Paper 1 develops the one-boundary IRFR admissibility framework and the exponential maturity-transport clock, implicitly under the Neumann or free short-end boundary condition. The present paper classifies the short-end boundary regimes generated by monetary-policy intervention. A sequel adds a stochastic realised long boundary and uses the Robin repeated-root loading derived here as the interior deformation coordinate in the completed two-boundary Jacobian.

The main claim is that policy intervention can be represented as a boundary condition at zero remaining maturity. 
\begin{itemize} 
\item A free short end corresponds to a Neumann-type boundary. 
\item A fully pinned policy rate corresponds to a Dirichlet boundary. 
\item Partial policy anchoring, in which the short end is influenced but not fixed by policy, corresponds to a Robin boundary. 
\end{itemize}
These boundary choices alter the admissible maturity profiles of drift, stochastic loading, and volatility across the curve.

The contribution is to show that humped volatility can arise endogenously from the boundary-value structure. In the repeated-root case $r=1$, the boundary problem produces the maturity loading $\tau e^{-a^2\tau}$, a linear-exponential form closely related to humped volatility specifications used in the term-structure literature often chosen because of its consistency with empirical observation. The hump, in this paper, is therefore not imposed as an external modelling choice; it is generated by the short-end boundary closure.

The paper develops this point in a term-structure setting built on instantaneous rolling forward rates. The first three IRFR axioms are retained: 
\begin{itemize}
\item stochastic evolution, 
\item rolling-maturity invariance, and 
\item equilibrium moment transport.  
\end{itemize}
The object that changes across policy regimes is the fourth condition: the boundary closure at zero remaining maturity.
In the non-intervention benchmark, the short end behaves as a free or natural boundary. Under a policy peg, it becomes a Dirichlet boundary. Under partial anchoring, it becomes a Robin boundary.

The Dirichlet case is useful precisely because it is too strong. It represents the limiting case in which the short rate is pinned at a policy value $x_D$. This is not a literal description of day-to-day monetary policy, but it provides a clean benchmark. If the short end is fixed, then stochastic variation cannot enter at zero maturity, any remaining uncertainty must be displaced into the interior of the curve. That displacement is the intuition behind the volatility hump.

The Robin case is the more realistic policy-intervention construct. In practice, policy does not pin the short end perfectly; it leans against short-rate moves. That is consistent with policy mandates that seek to moderate inflation, support growth, and stabilise financial conditions. A Robin boundary captures this by linking the short-end slope of the curve to the deviation of the short rate from the policy boundary. It is neither a fully free market boundary nor a perfectly fixed policy boundary.

The intended policy interpretation is therefore:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Neumann or free boundary:} the short end is market-determined and remains stochastic.
  \item \textbf{Dirichlet or fixed boundary:} the short end is pinned at a policy value $x_D$. This is a theoretical limiting case and a useful benchmark.
  \item \textbf{Robin or mixed boundary:} the short end is partially anchored to $x_D$. This is the preferred representation of policy intervention.
\end{itemize}

The paper's main results are as follows: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item First, boundary regimes imply different admissible maturity loadings.
    \item Second, the repeated-root $r=1$ branch generates the linear-exponential mode $\tau e^{-a^2\tau}$.
    \item Third, Robin anchoring gives the partial-policy regime between the free and fixed short-end cases.
    \item Fourth, the humped volatility profile appears as a consequence of the boundary-value problem rather than as an exogenous volatility specification.
\end{itemize}
    In the completed UBFT geometry, this Robin mode becomes the interior deformation loading that sits between short-boundary transport and long-boundary transport.

The rest of the paper is organised as follows. Section~2 summarises the IRFR machinery required for the boundary analysis. Sections~3--6 derive the Dirichlet construction and the repeated-root benchmark. Sections~7--10 develop the Robin boundary condition and its stochastic loading. Sections~11--13 relate the result to humped-volatility specifications and discuss signal-to-noise, total return, and total variance. The final section concludes and explains how the Robin repeated-root loading is used in the two-boundary sequel.

\section{Model setup and IRFR notation}

The underlying IRFR model derives affine drift and diffusion forms from rolling-maturity invariance and affine closure. It defines the ratio
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:r-ratio}
r:=\frac{\lambda^2}{a^2},
\end{equation}
which separates the maturity-decay scale $\lambda^2$ from the temporal drift-diffusion scale $a^2$. Within a given structural regime, the drift and diffusion share the same maturity loading. Consequently, the local market signal-to-noise ratio is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:within-regime-msnr}
\MSNR=\frac{a}{\sqrt{2}}.
\end{equation}

A key discipline in the boundary-condition paper is that the drift-diffusion scale is regime-indexed. We therefore write
\begin{equation}
a_N,\qquad a_D,\qquad a_R
\end{equation}
for Neumann, Dirichlet, and Robin regimes respectively. Cross-regime equality is not assumed in this paper.


\subsection{Notation convention for drift, diffusion, loading, and variance rate}

Throughout this note, the notation follows the IRFR convention in which $f$ denotes the drift-side coefficient in the Kolmogorov forward equation and $g$ denotes the half-volatility coefficient. Thus the It\^o volatility coefficient is
\begin{equation}
\sigma_B(\tau)=\sqrt{2}\,g_B(\tau),
\end{equation}
for a boundary regime $B\in\{N,D,R\}$. When the regime admits a maturity loading $H_B(\tau)$, we write
\begin{equation}
g_B(\tau)=a_BH_B(\tau),
\qquad
\sigma_B(\tau)=\sqrt{2}\,a_B|H_B(\tau)|.
\end{equation}
The corresponding local instantaneous variance rate is
\begin{equation}
v_B(\tau)
:=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Var(x^B_{t,t+\tau})
=2g_B(\tau)^2
=2a_B^2H_B(\tau)^2.
\end{equation}
This convention separates three objects that should not be conflated: $g_B$ is the half-volatility coefficient, $\sigma_B$ is the It\^o volatility coefficient, and $H_B$ is the maturity loading. The paper uses $v_B$ for the pointwise variance rate and reserves total variance for the rank-one integrated-loading object.

\subsection{Axioms 1--3 and the boundary closure condition}

The boundary-condition extension does not change the first three axioms of the IRFR framework. Axiom 1 supplies the diffusion setting; Axiom 2 imposes rolling-maturity invariance; and Axiom 3 governs equilibrium moment transport. These are the structural restrictions that make the model an IRFR model.

The point of departure is Axiom 4. In the original paper, Axiom 4 is stated as the positivity boundary at zero maturity: the short-maturity IRFR remains positive and the diffusion coefficient vanishes as the short-maturity rate approaches zero. That condition is appropriate for the non-intervention benchmark, where the short end is market-determined and the relevant lower boundary is zero.

In the present extension, Axiom 4 is better understood as a boundary closure condition rather than as a universal boundary for every policy regime. Monetary-policy intervention changes the short-end closure while leaving Axioms 1--3 intact. The original positivity boundary is therefore the Neumann/free-market closure; a fixed policy rate is the Dirichlet closure; and partial policy anchoring is the Robin closure.

This avoids treating policy intervention as an ad hoc change to the stochastic law. The stochastic-evolution, rolling-invariance, and moment-transport machinery remain the same. What changes is the admissible boundary behaviour at zero remaining maturity. Schematically:
\begin{align*}
\text{Non-intervention closure:} \quad & g_N(0,t,t)=0,\\
\text{Dirichlet policy closure:} \quad & x^D_{t,t}=x_D,\qquad f_D(x_D,t,t)=g_D(x_D,t,t)=0,\\
\text{Robin policy closure:} \quad & \left.\frac{\partial x^R_{t,t+\tau}}{\partial \tau}\right|_{\tau=0}=-\chi\bigl(x^R_{t,t}-x_D\bigr).
\end{align*}

The Dirichlet case should therefore be read as a clean limiting benchmark, not as a literal description of monetary policy. The Robin case is the more realistic intervention regime: policy attempts to moderate short-term interest-rate moves around an objective, reflecting mandates such as price stability and growth, but it does not eliminate all short-end randomness.

\section{Modelling conventions}

The analysis in this note uses the following modelling conventions, which are maintained throughout the remainder of the paper.
\begin{itemize}
  \item The repeated-root normalization is fixed as the theoretical coalescing-root benchmark. Empirical amplitude flexibility may be introduced separately through a multiplicative parameter $\eta_D$, but $\eta_D=1$ is the structural benchmark.
  \item The paper keeps $a_N$, $a_D$, and $a_R$ distinct throughout. It makes no common-$a$ comparison and does not assume cross-regime equality of MSNR or total variance.
  \item The Robin case is restricted to $r=1$ in this paper. A full general-$r$ Robin condition is a separate extension rather than part of the repeated-root bridge developed here.
  \item Axioms 1--3 are retained unchanged. Axiom 4 is treated as the boundary-regime closure condition: the original zero-positivity boundary is the non-intervention closure, while Dirichlet and Robin are policy-boundary closures.
  \item The notation follows the convention stated above: $g$ is the half-volatility coefficient with $\sigma=\sqrt{2}g$, and variance rates are written as $\partial_t\Var=2g^2$. The loading $H$ is the maturity profile satisfying $g_B=a_BH_B$ along the relevant branch.
\end{itemize}


\section{General Dirichlet construction for \texorpdfstring{$0<r_D\leq 1$}{0 < rD <= 1}}

Let $x_D$ denote the policy boundary imposed at the short end. Under the Dirichlet condition, the short end is fixed:
\begin{equation}
x^D_{t,t}=x_D.
\end{equation}
Use regime-specific parameters
\begin{equation}
a_D,\qquad r_D=\frac{\lambda_D^2}{a_D^2},\qquad 0<r_D\leq1.
\end{equation}

The Dirichlet analogue of the IRFR drift-diffusion pair is obtained by shifting the vanishing point of $f$ and $g$ from the original zero boundary to $x_D$. A convenient shifted coordinate is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dirichlet-shifted-coordinate}
\widetilde{x}^{D}_{t,T}
:=x_{t,T}-x_\infty-(x_D-x_\infty)e^{-r_Da_D^2(T-t)}.
\end{equation}
Then the Dirichlet drift and diffusion are
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dirichlet-fg-shifted}
f_D(x,t,T)=a_D^2\widetilde{x}^{D}_{t,T},
\qquad
 g_D(x,t,T)=a_D\widetilde{x}^{D}_{t,T}.
\end{equation}
Equivalently, written directly in the original state $x$,
\begin{align*}
f_D(x,t,T)&=a_D^2\left[x-x_\infty-(x_D-x_\infty)e^{-r_Da_D^2(T-t)}\right],\\
g_D(x,t,T)&=a_D\left[x-x_\infty-(x_D-x_\infty)e^{-r_Da_D^2(T-t)}\right].
\end{align*}
These satisfy the fixed-boundary conditions
\begin{equation}
f_D(x_D,t,t)=0,
\qquad
 g_D(x_D,t,t)=0.
\end{equation}

\section{Dirichlet term structure for general \texorpdfstring{$r_D$}{rD}}

The corresponding Dirichlet curve is the two-loading curve
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dirichlet-curve-general-r}
x^D_{t,t+\tau}
= x_\infty
+ (x_D-x_\infty)
\left[
\frac{1}{1+r_D}e^{-r_Da_D^2\tau}
+
\frac{r_D}{1+r_D}e^{-a_D^2\tau}
\right].
\end{equation}
It satisfies
\begin{equation}
x^D_{t,t}=x_D,
\qquad
\lim_{\tau\to\infty}x^D_{t,t+\tau}=x_\infty.
\end{equation}

For $0<r_D<1$, the two maturity loadings are genuinely distinct. The case $r_D=1$ is not obtained by simply inserting $r_D=1$ into this distinct-root formula. When the roots coalesce, the independent boundary mode changes form. This is why the case $r_D=1$ is developed separately below.

\paragraph{Reconciliation of curve normalizations.}
The curve above is the representation implied by the shifted-coordinate Dirichlet closure used in the previous section. It is obtained by choosing the Dirichlet equilibrium displacement so that the shifted coordinate vanishes at the policy boundary and the long-maturity limit is $x_\infty$.

An earlier candidate form,
\begin{equation}
x_\infty+(x_D-x_\infty)\left[(1+r)e^{-ra^2\tau}-re^{-a^2\tau}\right],
\end{equation}
uses a different amplitude normalization and a different sign convention for the second loading. Although it also satisfies the endpoint $x^D_{t,t}=x_D$, it is not the representation adopted here because it is not the one generated by the shifted coordinate $\widetilde{x}^D_{t,T}$. The paper therefore uses the plus-coefficient form throughout the distinct-root Dirichlet construction. This choice keeps the term structure, stochastic loading, and variance-rate formula aligned with the shifted-coordinate closure.

\section{Dirichlet instantaneous variance for general \texorpdfstring{$r_D$}{rD}}

Substituting the general Dirichlet curve in equation~\eqref{eq:dirichlet-curve-general-r} into the shifted coordinate in equation~\eqref{eq:dirichlet-shifted-coordinate} gives the distinct-root stochastic loading
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dirichlet-loading-general-r}
H_D^{(r)}(\tau)
=
\frac{r_D}{1+r_D}(x_D-x_\infty)
\left(e^{-a_D^2\tau}-e^{-r_Da_D^2\tau}\right).
\end{equation}
The substitution is direct. From Section 3, the shifted coordinate is
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{x}^D_{t,t+\tau}=x^D_{t,t+\tau}-x_\infty-(x_D-x_\infty)e^{-r_Da_D^2\tau}.
\end{equation}
Using the Section 4 curve, subtracting the boundary displacement leaves $H_D^{(r)}(\tau)$. Since $g_D=a_DH_D^{(r)}$, the variance-rate identity is
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Var(x^D_{t,t+\tau})=2g_D^2.
\end{equation}
Thus the local instantaneous variance rate associated with the loading in equation~\eqref{eq:dirichlet-loading-general-r} is
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Var(x^D_{t,t+\tau})
=
2a_D^2
\left(\frac{r_D}{1+r_D}\right)^2
(x_D-x_\infty)^2
\left(e^{-a_D^2\tau}-e^{-r_Da_D^2\tau}\right)^2.
\end{equation}
This expression vanishes at $\tau=0$ and as $\tau\to\infty$. It is the correct distinct-root Dirichlet formula for $0<r_D<1$. If $r_D$ is set equal to one inside this expression, the two exponentials cancel and the variance rate vanishes identically. That cancellation is a warning sign, not an economic result.

\section{The case \texorpdfstring{$r_D=1$}{rD=1} as a coalescing-root boundary problem}

The $r_D=1$ branch is not a generic point inside the Dirichlet family. It is a coalescing-root case. For $r_D\neq1$, the two independent exponential modes are
\begin{equation}
e^{-a_D^2\tau},
\qquad
 e^{-r_Da_D^2\tau}.
\end{equation}
As $r_D$ tends to one, these two modes collapse into the same exponential. The non-trivial second independent boundary mode is obtained by a difference quotient:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:coalescing-root-limit}
\frac{e^{-r_Da_D^2\tau}-e^{-a_D^2\tau}}{1-r_D}
\longrightarrow
 a_D^2\tau e^{-a_D^2\tau}.
\end{equation}

The rate at which the distinct-root loading degenerates can be made explicit. Start from
\begin{equation}
H_D^{(r)}(\tau)
=
\frac{r_D}{1+r_D}(x_D-x_\infty)
\left(e^{-a_D^2\tau}-e^{-r_Da_D^2\tau}\right).
\end{equation}
Let $r_D=1+\varepsilon$. Then, to first order in $\varepsilon$,
\begin{equation}
e^{-r_Da_D^2\tau}
=
e^{-a_D^2\tau}e^{-\varepsilon a_D^2\tau}
\approx
e^{-a_D^2\tau}\left(1-\varepsilon a_D^2\tau\right),
\end{equation}
and therefore
\begin{equation}
e^{-a_D^2\tau}-e^{-r_Da_D^2\tau}
\approx
\varepsilon a_D^2\tau e^{-a_D^2\tau},
\qquad
\frac{r_D}{1+r_D}\to \frac12.
\end{equation}
Hence
\begin{equation}
H_D^{(r)}(\tau)
\approx
\varepsilon\,\frac{a_D^2}{2}(x_D-x_\infty)\tau e^{-a_D^2\tau}.
\end{equation}
Thus the distinct-root loading vanishes linearly as $r_D\to1$, and the corresponding variance rate vanishes quadratically. The non-trivial repeated-root loading is obtained by extracting the Taylor coefficient at the coalescence point.

Equivalently, the repeated-root Dirichlet loading generated by the limit in equation~\eqref{eq:coalescing-root-limit} can be written as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dirichlet-repeated-root-loading}
H_D^{(1)}(\tau)=C_D\tau e^{-a_D^2\tau},
\end{equation}
with the theoretical repeated-root normalization
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dirichlet-cd-normalization}
C_D=\frac{a_D^2}{2}(x_D-x_\infty).
\end{equation}
The normalization in equation~\eqref{eq:dirichlet-cd-normalization} is fixed in the theoretical benchmark because the paper is deriving the humped loading from a boundary condition, not imposing an empirical amplitude. The sign of $C_D$ follows the convention used in the difference quotient. Reversing the quotient reverses the sign of $C_D$, but leaves the variance rate and local noise scale unchanged.

For empirical comparisons, the paper may introduce a separate multiplicative amplitude $\eta_D$:
\begin{equation}
H_{D,\mathrm{emp}}^{(1)}(\tau)=\eta_D C_D\tau e^{-a_D^2\tau}.
\end{equation}
The structural benchmark is $\eta_D=1$. Values not equal to one allow empirical amplitude distortion without weakening the theoretical claim that the shape is generated by the boundary condition.

The corresponding repeated-root variance-rate shape is
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Var(x^D_{t,t+\tau})
=
2a_D^2 C_D^2\tau^2 e^{-2a_D^2\tau}.
\end{equation}
This is the structural source of the volatility loading. It is generated by the fixed short-end boundary as a repeated-root mode; it is not imposed exogenously.

\section{Repeated-root benchmark: Neumann versus Dirichlet}

Before introducing Robin anchoring, it is useful to put the two limiting $r=1$ repeated-root benchmarks next to each other. The comparison shows exactly what the boundary condition changes. The Neumann case retains the ordinary exponential stochastic mode at the short end. The Dirichlet case removes that ordinary short-end stochastic mode and replaces it with the repeated-root boundary mode.

\paragraph{Neumann/free boundary.}
In the $r=1$ Neumann benchmark, write the equilibrium curve as
\begin{equation}
m_N(\tau)=x_\infty+A_Ne^{-a_N^2\tau}.
\end{equation}
The associated stochastic loading is
\begin{equation}
H_N(\tau)=A_Ne^{-a_N^2\tau},
\end{equation}
and the instantaneous variance rate is
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Var(x^N_{t,t+\tau})=2a_N^2A_N^2e^{-2a_N^2\tau}.
\end{equation}
The short-end volatility is non-zero whenever $A_N$ is non-zero. This is the free-boundary benchmark: stochastic exposure is allowed to enter immediately at zero maturity.

\paragraph{Dirichlet repeated-root boundary.}
In the $r=1$ Dirichlet benchmark, the equilibrium curve lies in the repeated-root linear-exponential family
\begin{equation}
m_D(\tau)=x_\infty+(A_D+B_D\tau)e^{-a_D^2\tau}.
\end{equation}
The pinned short-end condition gives
\begin{equation}
m_D(0)=x_\infty+A_D=x_D.
\end{equation}
The level condition therefore fixes
\begin{equation}
A_D=x_D-x_\infty.
\end{equation}
The coefficient $B_D$ controls the interior curvature of the repeated-root equilibrium curve unless an additional slope or calibration condition is imposed.

The corresponding repeated-root stochastic loading is
\begin{equation}
H_D(\tau)=C_D\tau e^{-a_D^2\tau},
\end{equation}
with the theoretical coalescing-root normalisation
\begin{equation}
C_D=\frac{a_D^2}{2}(x_D-x_\infty).
\end{equation}
Hence
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Var(x^D_{t,t+\tau})
=
2a_D^2C_D^2\tau^2e^{-2a_D^2\tau}.
\end{equation}
The Dirichlet boundary therefore forces the local volatility to vanish at $\tau=0$, while allowing stochastic exposure to appear inside the maturity curve.

The coefficients $B_D$ and $C_D$ play different roles. The coefficient $B_D$ determines the repeated-root component of the deterministic mean curve $m_D(\tau)$, while $C_D$ determines the repeated-root stochastic loading $H_D(\tau)$. The coalescing-root normalization fixes $C_D$ in the structural benchmark. By contrast, $B_D$ requires a separate slope condition, calibration choice, or economic normalization. The two coefficients should therefore not be identified unless an additional modelling restriction is imposed.

\paragraph{Expected values.}
The KFE first-moment identity gives the same conditional-expectation structure in each benchmark, with its own regime-specific equilibrium curve and drift-diffusion scale. For $B\in\{N,D\}$,
\begin{equation}
\E_t[x^B_{t+s,T}]
=
 m_B(T-t-s)
+
\left(x^B_{t,T}-m_B(T-t)\right)e^{-a_B^2s}.
\end{equation}
Thus the distinction between the two benchmarks is not the form of moment transport, but the admissible equilibrium curve and stochastic loading generated by the boundary condition.

Robin, developed next, interpolates between these limiting cases by retaining a residual short-end component while also allowing the boundary-induced repeated-root component.

\section{Robin repeated-root derivation and Ritchken--Chuang mapping}

The repeated-root loading provides the bridge to the more common exogenous humped-volatility specifications in the interest-rate literature. This section develops the Robin case from the repeated-root solution space before mapping it to Ritchken--Chuang-style notation.

A notational distinction is important. The Robin boundary strength is denoted by $\chi$. The exponential decay parameter in the empirical humped-volatility notation is denoted by $\gamma$. This avoids using the same symbol for two conceptually different objects.

In the $r=1$ repeated-root branch, the independent maturity modes are
\begin{equation}
e^{-a_R^2\tau},
\qquad
\tau e^{-a_R^2\tau}.
\end{equation}
Therefore any Robin loading or equilibrium curve built in this branch should live in the linear-exponential span generated by these two functions.

The restriction to $r=1$ in the Robin development is deliberate rather than merely incomplete. It is the minimal repeated-root setting in which the boundary-induced hump appears in linear-exponential form, making the connection to the standard humped-volatility literature transparent. A full general-$r$ Robin theory would be a separate extension, not the object of the present bridge paper.

\section{Robin mean curve and boundary condition}

Let $a_R$ denote the Robin drift-diffusion scale. A compatible Robin equilibrium or mean curve is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:robin-mean-curve}
m_R(\tau)=x_\infty+(A_R+B_R\tau)e^{-a_R^2\tau}.
\end{equation}
At the short end,
\begin{equation}
m_R(0)=x_\infty+A_R.
\end{equation}
Differentiating gives
\begin{equation}
m_R'(\tau)=\left[B_R-a_R^2(A_R+B_R\tau)\right]e^{-a_R^2\tau},
\end{equation}
and hence
\begin{equation}
m_R'(0)=B_R-a_R^2A_R.
\end{equation}
The Robin boundary condition links the short-end slope to the deviation of the short-end curve from the policy boundary $x_D$:
\begin{equation}
m_R'(0)=-\chi\bigl(m_R(0)-x_D\bigr).
\end{equation}
Substituting the value and derivative at the boundary gives the coefficient restriction
\begin{equation}
B_R-a_R^2A_R=-\chi(x_\infty+A_R-x_D).
\end{equation}
Equivalently,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:robin-boundary-coefficient}
B_R=a_R^2A_R-\chi(x_\infty+A_R-x_D).
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:robin-boundary-coefficient} also gives a convenient formula for the shape of a Robin IRFR curve at calendar time $t$. Write the current short-end value as
\begin{equation}
x^R_{t,t}=x_R(t),
\end{equation}
and set
\begin{equation}
A_R(t):=x_R(t)-x_\infty.
\end{equation}
The repeated-root Robin curve is then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:robin-irfr-curve-ab}
x^R_{t,t+\tau}
=
x_\infty+\left[A_R(t)+B_R(t)\tau\right]e^{-a_R^2\tau},
\end{equation}
where the Robin boundary condition determines the slope coefficient as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:robin-slope-coefficient}
B_R(t)=a_R^2\bigl(x_R(t)-x_\infty\bigr)-\chi\bigl(x_R(t)-x_D\bigr).
\end{equation}
Equivalently, the curve may be written directly in terms of the current short rate:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:robin-irfr-curve}
x^R_{t,t+\tau}
=
x_\infty+
\left[
\bigl(x^R_{t,t}-x_\infty\bigr)
+
\left\{
a_R^2\bigl(x^R_{t,t}-x_\infty\bigr)-\chi\bigl(x^R_{t,t}-x_D\bigr)
\right\}\tau
\right]
e^{-a_R^2\tau}.
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:robin-irfr-curve} is the Robin analogue of the repeated-root IRFR curve shape. It starts from the observed short-end value, imposes the mixed boundary restriction at $\tau=0$, and converges to $x_\infty$ as $\tau\to\infty$.

\paragraph{Illustration: the neutral boundary $x_D=x_\infty/2$.}
A useful benchmark is to set the policy boundary equal to the $r=1$ non-intervention short-end equilibrium,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:neutral-boundary}
x_D=\frac{x_\infty}{2}.
\end{equation}
Then the Robin slope coefficient becomes
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:robin-slope-neutral-boundary}
B_R(t)=a_R^2\bigl(x_R(t)-x_\infty\bigr)-\chi\left(x_R(t)-\frac{x_\infty}{2}\right).
\end{equation}
The second term is the policy-boundary response. If the short end lies above the neutral boundary, $x_R(t)>x_\infty/2$, the Robin term reduces the initial slope relative to the unconstrained repeated-root component. If the short end lies below the neutral boundary, $x_R(t)<x_\infty/2$, the Robin term increases the initial slope. Under the sign convention used in this paper, this can be described as a loosening response when the curve is above the neutral boundary and a tightening response when the curve is below it. If an empirical implementation uses the opposite sign convention for the IRFR state, these labels should be reversed; the invariant object is the boundary-response term $-\chi(x_R(t)-x_D)$.

This restriction is one equation in two coefficients. Thus the Robin mean curve is a one-parameter family unless an additional normalisation is imposed. In this interpretation, $A_R$ controls the short-end level $m_R(0)$, while $\chi$ controls how strongly the initial slope reacts to the deviation of that short-end level from the policy boundary. Empirical or policy calibration may choose $A_R$ directly; the Robin boundary condition then determines $B_R$.

Thus Robin is a partial-policy boundary. It does not force $m_R(0)=x_D$. Instead, it says that when the short end deviates from the policy boundary, the initial slope of the curve responds in a direction that moderates that deviation. Economically, $\chi$ is the policy reaction strength at the short-end boundary. A small $\chi$ corresponds to weak anchoring and a boundary closer to the free-market case. A large $\chi$ corresponds to strong policy correction: deviations from $x_D$ generate a steeper boundary response. In the formal anchoring limit $\chi\to\infty$, with $m_R(0)\to x_D$, the Robin boundary approaches the Dirichlet fixed-boundary benchmark.

\section{Robin stochastic loading and instantaneous variance}

The stochastic loading should be built in the same repeated-root function space as the mean curve. Write
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:robin-loading}
H_R(\tau)=(C_0+C_1\tau)e^{-a_R^2\tau}.
\end{equation}
Here $C_0$ is residual short-end stochastic loading and $C_1\tau$ is the boundary-induced or policy-transition loading. The Dirichlet limit corresponds to $C_0=0$; a non-zero $C_0$ means the short end remains partly stochastic.

\begin{proposition}[Boundary-induced linear-exponential volatility mode]
In the repeated-root branch $r=1$, a Dirichlet or Robin short-end boundary generates a linear-exponential stochastic loading. In the Dirichlet limiting case, the loading is proportional to $\tau e^{-a_D^2\tau}$. In the Robin case, the admissible loading takes the form $(C_0+C_1\tau)e^{-a_R^2\tau}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
For $r_D\neq1$, the distinct-root Dirichlet modes are $e^{-a_D^2\tau}$ and $e^{-r_Da_D^2\tau}$. As $r_D$ tends to one, these modes coalesce. The non-trivial second mode is obtained by extracting the first-order Taylor coefficient of their difference, giving $\tau e^{-a_D^2\tau}$. The Robin boundary retains the same repeated-root solution space but allows a residual short-end component in addition to the boundary-induced component. Hence the Robin loading is linear-exponential. This proves that the humped volatility shape is generated by the boundary condition rather than imposed as a primitive volatility function.
\end{proof}

The Dirichlet repeated-root case is recovered from the Robin loading by setting $C_0=0$ and $C_1=C_D$. At the boundary-condition level it is recovered as the strong-anchoring limit $\chi\to\infty$, with $m_R(0)\to x_D$, so that the short end is forced onto the policy boundary. Robin is therefore genuinely an interpolating construction: finite $\chi$ permits partial anchoring, while the limiting case recovers the fixed-boundary benchmark.

It is important not to collapse the state-dependent drift into a maturity-only object too early. The structural affine Robin drift should be written as
\begin{equation}
f_R(x,t,T)=a_R^2\left[x-m_R(\tau)\right]+m_R'(\tau),
\qquad \tau=T-t.
\end{equation}
The volatility loading is represented separately by $H_R(\tau)$. Along the relevant mean/loading branch, the local signal and noise share this same $H_R$.

The diffusion coefficient in the IRFR convention is
\begin{equation}
g_R(\tau)=a_RH_R(\tau),
\end{equation}
and since $\sigma_R=\sqrt{2}\,g_R$, the local volatility is
\begin{equation}
\sigma_R(\tau)=\sqrt{2}\,a_R\left|H_R(\tau)\right|.
\end{equation}
The instantaneous variance rate is therefore
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Var(x^R_{t,t+\tau})
=
2a_R^2\left(C_0+C_1\tau\right)^2e^{-2a_R^2\tau}.
\end{equation}
This is the structural Robin variance form: the loading is mixed first, and the variance is the square of that mixed loading.

The mechanism is therefore simple. The boundary suppresses, or partially suppresses, stochastic loading at $\tau=0$. Because diffusion still operates through the curve, the stochastic exposure is redistributed away from the short end. The result is a front-end loading when policy is absent, a pure interior hump in the Dirichlet limit, and a mixed front-end-plus-hump profile under Robin anchoring.

\subsection{Admissibility and positivity of repeated-root curves}

A boundary condition fixes or constrains the behaviour at zero maturity, but it does not by itself guarantee that the entire repeated-root curve remains positive over the maturity domain. For the repeated-root Dirichlet and Robin cases, positivity is therefore an admissibility condition on the coefficients of the linear-exponential curve.

Consider a generic repeated-root mean curve
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:admissibility-generic-curve}
m(\tau)=x_\infty+(A+B\tau)e^{-a^2\tau}.
\end{equation}
Differentiating with respect to maturity gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:admissibility-first-derivative}
m'(\tau)=\left[B-a^2(A+B\tau)\right]e^{-a^2\tau}.
\end{equation}
Since the exponential term is strictly positive, an interior stationary point satisfies
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:admissibility-stationary-point}
\tau^* = \frac{B-a^2A}{a^2B},
\qquad B\neq0,
\end{equation}
provided $\tau^*>0$. The stationary point is a maximum when $B>0$ and a minimum when $B<0$.

The positivity restriction is only non-trivial when the repeated-root curve has an interior minimum. Thus suppose
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:admissibility-minimum-conditions}
B<0,
\qquad
\tau_{\min}=\frac{B-a^2A}{a^2B}>0.
\end{equation}
At the minimum,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:admissibility-A-plus-Btau}
A+B\tau_{\min}=\frac{B}{a^2}.
\end{equation}
Therefore
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:admissibility-minimum-value}
m(\tau_{\min})
=
x_\infty+\frac{B}{a^2}
\exp\left(-1+\frac{a^2A}{B}\right).
\end{equation}
The repeated-root curve is admissible only if the interior minimum remains positive:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:admissibility-positivity-condition}
x_\infty+\frac{B}{a^2}
\exp\left(-1+\frac{a^2A}{B}\right)>0.
\end{equation}
Equivalently, for $B<0$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:admissibility-positivity-condition-absolute}
\frac{|B|}{a^2}
\exp\left(-1+\frac{a^2A}{B}\right)<x_\infty.
\end{equation}
This condition formalises the admissibility restriction used in the stylised example below: the Robin boundary may generate an interior trough, but the parameters must keep that trough above zero.

For the stylised Robin sign convention used in the figures below,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:admissibility-robin-A-B}
A_j=x_j-x_\infty,
\qquad
B_j=a_R^2(x_j-x_\infty)+\chi(x_j-x_D).
\end{equation}
The below-equilibrium case has $B_-<0$ and hence an interior minimum. The illustrative parameter choice in equation~\eqref{eq:stylised-robin-parameters} gives $\tau_{\min}\approx5.1$ years and $m(\tau_{\min})\approx0.46\%$, so the plotted curve satisfies the positivity condition in equation~\eqref{eq:admissibility-positivity-condition}.

\subsection{Stylised 80/20 Robin curve and volatility illustrations}

The neutral-boundary convention in equation~\eqref{eq:neutral-boundary} can be illustrated by choosing three short-end states
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:stylised-robin-states}
x_-<x_D,
\qquad
x_0=x_D,
\qquad
x_+>x_D.
\end{equation}
Here $x_-$ is interpreted as the below-equilibrium or tightening case, $x_0$ as the neutral case, and $x_+$ as the above-equilibrium or loosening case. To make the illustration concrete, the figures use
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:stylised-robin-parameters}
x_\infty=4\%,
\qquad
x_D=\frac{x_\infty}{2}=2\%,
\qquad
x_-=1.5\%,
\qquad
x_0=2.0\%,
\qquad
x_+=2.5\%,
\qquad
a_R=0.35,
\qquad
\chi=1.
\end{equation}

For the stylised yield-curve plot, the Robin repeated-root curve is written as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:stylised-robin-curve-general}
x^R_j(\tau)
=
x_\infty+(A_j+B_j\tau)e^{-a_R^2\tau},
\qquad j\in\{-,0,+\},
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:stylised-robin-ab}
A_j:=x_j-x_\infty,
\qquad
B_j:=a_R^2(x_j-x_\infty)+s\chi(x_j-x_D).
\end{equation}
The sign parameter $s$ records the convention used to label movements around the policy boundary. The figures use $s=+1$, so that an above-equilibrium short end generates an upward boundary response and can produce an interior maximum in the IRFR curve. This sign convention is illustrative; it is the direction of the boundary-response term, rather than the label attached to it, that carries the mathematical content.

With the parameters in equation~\eqref{eq:stylised-robin-parameters}, the below-equilibrium curve remains positive and has an interior minimum of approximately $0.46\%$ at about $5.1$ years. The above-equilibrium curve has an interior maximum of approximately $4.53\%$ at about $12.9$ years. The neutral curve is monotone in the displayed maturity range. These turning points follow from differentiating equation~\eqref{eq:stylised-robin-curve-general}: if $B_j\neq 0$, the stationary maturity is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:stylised-robin-turning-point}
\tau^*_j=
\frac{B_j-a_R^2A_j}{a_R^2B_j},
\end{equation}
provided $\tau^*_j>0$. The stationary point is a maximum when $B_j>0$ and a minimum when $B_j<0$.

For the matching stylised volatility curves, the example uses an $80\%$ Neumann and $20\%$ Dirichlet mixture of the repeated-root loadings. Let
\begin{equation}

\end{equation}
where $C_{N,j}$ is the residual market, or Neumann, loading in state $j$ and $C_D\tau$ is the boundary-induced repeated-root, or Dirichlet, loading. The associated illustrative volatility is

=
\sqrt{2}\,a_R\left|H^{80/20}_j(\tau)\right|.
\end{equation}
This is a plotting convention rather than a calibration restriction. It makes explicit the interpretation of Robin as a partial-boundary case: most of the stochastic loading remains market-like, but a smaller repeated-root component generates an interior hump.

\section{Mapping to Ritchken--Chuang-style humped volatility}

The humped-volatility literature typically begins by specifying a maturity-dependent volatility function. Ritchken and Chuang introduce stationary humped forward-rate volatility in an HJM setting. Moraleda and Vorst and Mercurio and Moraleda develop tractable humped-volatility models for interest-rate claims, while Falini documents cap-pricing benefits from humped HJM volatility specifications. The present paper differs from this literature by deriving a linear-exponential humped loading from a boundary condition. The mapping below is therefore not only a change of notation; it identifies the reduced-form humped-volatility parameters with the coefficients of a repeated-root Robin boundary structure.

A common reduced-form humped-volatility notation can be written as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rc-volatility}
\sigma_{RC}(\tau)=(\alpha+\beta\tau)e^{-\gamma\tau}.
\end{equation}
Using the Robin loading in equation~\eqref{eq:robin-loading} gives
\begin{equation}
\sigma_R(\tau)
=
\sqrt{2}a_R(C_0+C_1\tau)e^{-a_R^2\tau},
\end{equation}
whenever $C_0+C_1\tau$ is non-negative on the maturity range. The mapping is then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rc-robin-mapping}
\alpha=\sqrt{2}a_RC_0,
\qquad
\beta=\sqrt{2}a_RC_1,
\qquad
\gamma=a_R^2.
\end{equation}
For the pure Dirichlet repeated-root case, $C_0=0$ and $C_1=C_D$, so the loading reduces to
\begin{equation}
\sigma_D(\tau)=\sqrt{2}a_DC_D\tau e^{-a_D^2\tau},
\end{equation}
and therefore
\begin{equation}
\alpha=0,
\qquad
\beta=\sqrt{2}a_DC_D,
\qquad
\gamma=a_D^2.
\end{equation}

The cleanest empirical comparison is obtained under the following restrictions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\gamma>0$, so the exponential decay scale is positive.
  \item $\alpha\geq0$ and $\beta\geq0$, so the Robin loading is non-negative over all $\tau\geq0$.
  \item For a genuine interior hump with $\alpha>0$, require $\beta>\gamma\alpha$. The volatility peak is
  \begin{equation}
  \tau^* = \frac{\beta-\gamma\alpha}{\gamma\beta}.
  \end{equation}
  \item For the pure Dirichlet repeated-root case $\alpha=0$, the peak is
  \begin{equation}
  \tau^*=\frac{1}{\gamma}=\frac{1}{a_D^2}.
  \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

This is the central empirical bridge. The Ritchken--Chuang-style formulation specifies a humped volatility function directly. The boundary-condition interpretation derives the same linear-exponential loading from the repeated-root Robin or Dirichlet boundary structure.

\section{Expected value under the Robin repeated-root branch: KFE derivation}

The Robin expected-value formula follows from the KFE first-moment identity, not merely from analogy with the $r=1$ transport formula.

Let
\begin{equation}
\tau=T-t,
\end{equation}
and let the Robin equilibrium curve be
\begin{equation}
m_R(\tau)=x_\infty+(A_R+B_R\tau)e^{-a_R^2\tau}.
\end{equation}
Choose the Robin drift in the structural affine form
\begin{equation}
f_R(x,t,T)=a_R^2\left[x-m_R(\tau)\right]+m_R'(\tau).
\end{equation}
The KFE first-moment identity gives
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\E[x^R_{t,T}]
=-\E[f_R(x^R_{t,T},t,T)].
\end{equation}
Taking expectations in the affine drift gives
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\E[x^R_{t,T}]
=-a_R^2\left(\E[x^R_{t,T}]-m_R(\tau)\right)-m_R'(\tau).
\end{equation}
Because $\tau=T-t$, the equilibrium branch satisfies
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}m_R(\tau)=-m_R'(\tau).
\end{equation}
Define the deviation
\begin{equation}
z(t):=\E[x^R_{t,T}]-m_R(T-t).
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dd z(t)}{\dd t}
=-a_R^2z(t).
\end{equation}
Solving from $t_0$ to $t$ gives
\begin{equation}
z(t)=z(t_0)e^{-a_R^2(t-t_0)}.
\end{equation}
Therefore the conditional expectation is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:robin-expectation}
\E_{t_0}[x^R_{t,T}]
=
 m_R(T-t)
+
\left(x^R_{t_0,T}-m_R(T-t_0)\right)e^{-a_R^2(t-t_0)}.
\end{equation}
This is the rigorous Robin expectation formula. It follows directly from the KFE moment identity and the affine Robin drift, with the repeated-root equilibrium curve $m_R(\tau)$.

\section{MSNR, total return, and total variance under Robin}

Within the Robin regime, the local signal and local noise share the same loading $H_R$. The signal scale is
\begin{equation}
S_R(\tau)=a_R^2H_R(\tau),
\end{equation}
while the local volatility scale is
\begin{equation}
\sqrt{v_R(\tau)}=\sqrt{2}a_R|H_R(\tau)|.
\end{equation}
Thus, where $H_R(\tau)$ is non-zero,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:robin-msnr}
\MSNR_R(\tau)=\frac{|S_R(\tau)|}{\sqrt{v_R(\tau)}}=\frac{a_R}{\sqrt{2}}.
\end{equation}
The MSNR is maturity-independent within the Robin regime. However, no equality between $a_N$, $a_D$, and $a_R$ is assumed. Cross-regime MSNR comparisons require an explicit additional restriction, which this paper does not impose.

This is a within-regime scalar Robin statement. In the two-boundary extension, when the Robin channel is coupled to a stochastic long-boundary channel, the correlated two-channel MSNR is computed from the inverse covariance matrix before the channels are collapsed into a scalar variance norm. The present result should therefore be read as the one-channel Robin clock that the sequel embeds in a larger two-boundary covariance geometry.

Because the IRFR covariance-rate kernel is rank one, the variance of an integrated maturity object is the square of the integrated stochastic loading. One should integrate volatility loadings first and then square the result, rather than integrating pointwise variances:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{tot}}_R
=
2a_R^2\left(\int_0^\infty |H_R(\tau)|\dd\tau\right)^2.
\end{equation}
In the Robin repeated-root case,
\begin{equation}
\int_0^\infty H_R(\tau)\dd\tau
=
\frac{C_0}{a_R^2}+\frac{C_1}{a_R^4},
\end{equation}
provided a consistent sign convention is used for $H_R$. If $H_R$ has a consistent sign over the relevant maturity region, then
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{tot}}_R
=
2a_R^2\left(\frac{C_0}{a_R^2}+\frac{C_1}{a_R^4}\right)^2.
\end{equation}
The corresponding total instantaneous expected return is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{tot}}_R
=
\int_0^\infty a_R^2H_R(\tau)\dd\tau
=
C_0+\frac{C_1}{a_R^2}.
\end{equation}
Consequently, within a fixed Robin regime,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{tot}}_R}{\sqrt{\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{tot}}_R}}
=
\frac{a_R}{\sqrt{2}},
\end{equation}
again assuming a consistent sign convention for the loading. This is an internal Robin-regime statement, not a claim of equality across Neumann, Dirichlet, and Robin regimes.


\section{Conclusion}

This paper interprets monetary-policy intervention as a boundary condition on the IRFR curve. The first three IRFR axioms - stochastic evolution, rolling-maturity invariance, and equilibrium moment transport - are retained. What changes across policy regimes is the short-end boundary closure.

The general Dirichlet construction shows how a fixed policy boundary changes the admissible maturity loadings of the forward-rate system. For $0<r_D<1$, the Dirichlet branch has two distinct exponential modes. The branch $r_D=1$ is different: it is a coalescing-root case in which the non-trivial boundary-induced mode is obtained by extracting the repeated-root limit. This produces the linear-exponential loading
\begin{equation}
\tau e^{-a_D^2\tau}.
\end{equation}

The repeated-root loading gives a structural interpretation of humped volatility forms that are usually specified exogenously. In this interpretation, the volatility hump is not a primitive modelling choice. It is the maturity-domain shadow of a fixed or partially fixed short-end boundary.

The Robin construction then provides the realistic partial-anchoring case. Its mean curve and stochastic loading both live in the repeated-root span generated by $e^{-a_R^2\tau}$ and $\tau e^{-a_R^2\tau}$. The Robin boundary leaves some residual short-end stochasticity while allowing policy-induced displacement of stochastic exposure into the interior of the curve. This gives a direct mapping to Ritchken--Chuang-style linear-exponential humped volatility, while preserving the IRFR discipline that $a_N$, $a_D$, and $a_R$ remain boundary-regime-specific drift-diffusion scales.

The main message is therefore that monetary policy can be modelled as a boundary operator on the IRFR system. Boundary conditions do not merely alter the short end locally; they determine the admissible shape of volatility across the maturity curve.

\section*{Relationship to prior work}

This paper uses the rolling-maturity term-structure machinery developed in Oza~\cite{Oza2026IRFR}. The source results used here are the general $r$-branch, the affine drift-diffusion form, the instantaneous variance-rate formula, the constant within-regime MSNR result, and the rank-one covariance aggregation logic for integrated maturity objects. The present contribution is to reinterpret monetary-policy intervention as a boundary closure and to show that the repeated-root boundary problem generates humped maturity loadings structurally. The Ritchken--Chuang connection is developed as a literature bridge: the boundary-condition approach derives a humped loading from the short-end boundary condition, whereas much of the earlier literature specifies humped forward-rate volatility functions directly.

In the UBFT sequence, this paper supplies the short-end boundary classification used in the stochastic long-boundary completion. The Robin loading $\tau e^{-a_R^2\tau}$ derived here is the interior deformation coordinate in the completed three-loading maturity geometry.
